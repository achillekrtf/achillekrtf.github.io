Every five minutes, Polymarket opens a new market on one question: will Bitcoin be higher at the end of the next five minutes than at the start? That makes 288 markets a day on BTC alone, about $6.9M of trading per day, and a market structure that looks made for a market maker: retail-sized orders (the median trade is $4.55), a known resolution source, and a fee schedule that pays makers and charges takers.

I built a bot for these markets earlier this year: a fair value from the Binance spot price, inventory skew, adverse-selection filters. This article asks the question that should have come first: could a two-sided quoter make money here at all, and how fast would it need to be?

## How the markets work

| | |
|---|---|
| Resolution | Up if Chainlink's BTC/USD 60-second TWAP at the end of the window is at or above its value at the start |
| Window | 300 seconds; trading opens hours before the window starts |
| Taker fee | 0.07 × p × (1 − p) per share: 1.75¢ per share at a price of 50¢, which is 3.5% of the notional |
| Maker fee | None. Makers receive 20% of taker fees as rebates |

The resolution rule matters for the model. Polymarket's description only says "TWAP of the time range ... greater than or equal to the price at the beginning". I tested four readings of it against Binance data. Comparing the average price of the last minute with the average of the minute before the window opened matched 97.5% of the 8,640 real outcomes; the next best reading matched 87.0%. The remaining 2.5% are near-ties where Binance and Chainlink disagree. In practice, the reference price is set before the window opens, and only the last minute decides the result.

## Data

<Metrics items={[["8,640", "Markets, 5 Sep to 4 Oct 2026"], ["12.9M", "Taker trades"], ["$205M", "Traded notional"], ["$3.86M", "Taker fees paid"]]} />

- **Trades:** every taker trade in every BTC 5-minute market over 30 days, from Polymarket's public Data API (side, outcome, price, size, timestamp). Trades on the Down token are converted to the equivalent trade on Up, so every market has a single price.
- **Outcomes:** from Polymarket's Gamma API. 50.3% of markets resolved Up.
- **Spot:** Binance BTC/USDT one-second closes over the same period.
- **Fair value:** at each second, the probability that the last-minute average ends above the reference, with BTC modelled as a random walk using the realised volatility of the previous 30 minutes.

## Who makes money

Takers lost about $4.0M over the month: $3.86M in fees and $170k to the makers on the other side. Polymarket kept $3.09M after paying $0.77M of rebates. Fees averaged 188 basis points of traded notional, which is larger than any edge discussed below.

To measure what makers earned, I took the other side of every taker trade and compared its price with my spot-based fair value at the moment of the fill, a few seconds later, and at resolution.

![Maker P&L vs fair value at the fill, 5 and 30 seconds later, and at resolution](/images/polymarket-mm/markout.png)

- **At the fill, makers trade 108 bp better than fair value.** Takers pay a wide spread.
- **Most of it is gone within 30 seconds.** The price moves in the taker's direction after the trade. That is adverse selection: takers who trade aggressively tend to know where BTC just moved.
- **At resolution, makers keep 5.5 bp**, with a 95% confidence interval of −9 to +20 bp (bootstrap over markets, since trades in one market share one outcome). Without rebates, market making here is roughly break-even.
- **Rebates add 36 bp**, bringing makers to +42 bp (27 to 56). The edge that survives is mostly the fee subsidy.

My spot model also turned out to be a worse forecaster than the market. On in-window trades, the Brier score of the traded price was 0.162 against 0.170 for the model, and the market's prices were well calibrated in every 10-cent bucket. Whoever sets these prices already sees Binance and reacts to it.

## A simulated quoter

The aggregate numbers describe makers who are first in the queue. I wanted to know what a new quoter would earn, so I simulated one on every market.

| Setting | Value |
|---|---|
| Center | Either my spot fair value, or an EWMA of recent trade prices |
| Quotes | Bid and ask at center ± 1¢, 2¢, 3¢ or 5¢, rounded to the tick, skewed by inventory |
| Size | 20 shares per quote, inventory capped at 100 shares, held to resolution |
| Fills | When a taker trade prints at my price (or, in the strict version, through it), up to the traded size |
| Latency | Quotes use information from L seconds before each trade |
| Stops | No quotes in the last 15 seconds, or when the center is below 4¢ or above 96¢ |

The first run produced a winner. Quoting ±5¢ around the spot fair value with a one-second latency made $36,300 over 30 days, positive on 73% of days. Spreads of 2¢ or less lost, the trade-following quoter lost everywhere, and three seconds of latency turned every configuration negative.

That cliff between one and three seconds was suspicious.

## The 2-second trap

The Data API's timestamp is the time the trade settled on Polygon. The trade was matched off-chain a little earlier. If that gap is large, a "one-second latency" in my simulation means quoting with spot prices from after the trade was already matched.

I checked it two ways. First, I shifted the spot model in time and measured how well its changes explain changes in trade prices.

![Correlation between trade price changes and spot-model changes, by time shift](/images/polymarket-mm/timestamp-lag.png)

The correlation peaks when the spot model is shifted 3 seconds earlier (0.58, against 0.09 with no shift). Either the market reacts to Binance with a 3-second lag, which would be a real opportunity, or the timestamps are late, which would be a bug in my backtest.

Second, I recorded one live market through Polymarket's websocket, which reports trades at match time, and matched all 394 trades to the Data API by transaction hash. The API timestamps were 2.2 seconds late on average (from 1.0 to 3.5 seconds). Add half a second for the alignment of one-second Binance candles, and the 3-second peak is fully explained. The market reacts to Binance in well under a second.

With that delay subtracted, the latency curve reads differently.

![Simulated quoter P&L per day against real quote latency](/images/polymarket-mm/latency.png)

| Quoter (±5¢, fill at my price) | Real latency | P&L per day | bp of my volume |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spot fair value | −1.2 s (looks ahead) | +$1,211 | +240 |
| Spot fair value | −0.2 s (looks ahead) | +$277 | +57 |
| Spot fair value | 0.8 s | −$868 | −174 |
| Spot fair value | 2.8 s | −$3,266 | −554 |
| Trade-following | 0.8 s | −$3,489 | −597 |

Every configuration with a realistic latency loses money, across all four spreads and both fill rules. The only profitable rows are the ones where the simulation knew where BTC went after the trade.

## What I take from it

- **The spread is real, and it belongs to whoever is fastest.** Makers capture 108 bp at the fill and lose most of it within seconds. A quoter that updates within a second still gets picked off when BTC moves.
- **Rebates are most of the surviving edge.** Without them, makers as a group break even within the confidence interval.
- **Check timestamps before trusting a latency result.** A 2.2-second reporting delay produced a strategy with a 73% daily win rate. Measuring the delay against a live feed took one market and ten minutes.
- **The fees fall on takers.** At 188 bp on average, a taker strategy needs a large and very fast edge before it breaks even.

I set out to tune a two-sided quoter for these markets. The backtest points to speed instead: at retail infrastructure speeds, the fills you get are the ones you did not want.

## Methodology and limits

- **One-second resolution.** Trades, quotes and spot are all aligned to the second, so the simulation cannot represent sub-second competition. Professional makers here operate in milliseconds.
- **Queue position is ignored.** A fill at my price assumes I was first in line at that level. The strict version, which requires the trade to go through my price, is more conservative and loses more.
- **Rebates are approximated** as 20% of the fee each fill generated. Polymarket distributes the pool daily, and the exact allocation may differ.
- **The fair value uses Binance as a proxy for Chainlink**, which matches 97.5% of outcomes. The disagreements are concentrated near 50/50, where they matter most.
- **One month of data.** The Data API only keeps about a month of trade history, so this covers 5 September to 4 October 2026. Some prices in the API are averages across several levels of the book (about 11% of trades); I use the average as given, which is less extreme than the worst level reached and so errs toward fewer fills.
