Every five minutes, Polymarket opens a market on a single question: will Bitcoin be higher at the end of the next five minutes than at the start? There are 288 of them a day, and together they trade about $7 million. The orders are small, typically a few dollars, and the fee schedule charges takers while paying makers a rebate. On paper, it is a market built for someone who posts quotes on both sides and collects the spread.

Earlier this year I wrote a bot to do exactly that. It priced each market from the Binance spot price, skewed its quotes when it held inventory, and tried to step aside when the flow looked informed. What I never did was check whether the idea could work at all. So I went back and did it properly, on a full month of data.

## How these markets actually resolve

The first surprise came before any trading logic. Polymarket's rules say the market resolves on Chainlink's "TWAP" of the time range, compared with the price at the beginning. That sentence can be read several ways, and the reading matters, because it decides what a fair price is at any moment.

So I tested the readings against reality. Using Binance prices, I computed each candidate rule for all 8,640 markets between 5 September and 4 October and compared it with the official outcome. One reading stood out: the average price over the last minute of the window, compared with the average over the minute before the window opened. It matched 97.5% of the real results. The next best interpretation managed 87%, and the few misses were coin flips that Binance and Chainlink saw slightly differently.

That changes how you think about the market. The reference price is locked in before the window even starts, and only the final minute counts. The first four minutes matter only because of where they leave Bitcoin going into the last one.

## Who makes money

I pulled every trade from Polymarket's public API: almost 13 million of them over the month, for about $205 million of volume. The first thing the data shows is where the money goes. Takers paid $3.9 million in fees. The fee is largest when the price sits near 50 cents, where it costs 1.75 cents per share, or 3.5% of what you pay. Polymarket gave about a fifth of that back to makers as rebates and kept the rest.

The makers on the other side of those trades did well in the first instant and much less well afterwards. I compared each fill with a fair value built from the Binance price. At the moment of the trade, makers were buying about one percent below fair value and selling about one percent above. Thirty seconds later, half of that edge was gone. By the time the market resolved, almost nothing was left.

![Maker P&L vs fair value at the fill, 5 and 30 seconds later, and at resolution](/images/polymarket-mm/markout.png)

This is adverse selection in its plainest form. The people who cross the spread are often reacting to a move in Bitcoin that the resting quotes have not caught up with yet. Once you add the rebates, makers as a group end up slightly positive, but the rebate is doing most of the work. Without it, making markets here is close to break-even.

There was a second, humbler finding. My fair value was a worse forecaster than the market itself. Whoever sets these prices already sees Binance, and reacts to it faster than my model does.

## A quoter that looked too good

Knowing what makers earn on average does not tell you what a newcomer would earn, because a newcomer is rarely first in line. So I simulated one. It quoted around my fair value, held whatever it bought until the market resolved, and got filled whenever a real trade printed at its price. I tried several spreads and several reaction delays.

The first run produced a winner. Quoting five cents either side of fair value, with one second of delay, it made about $36,000 over the month and was profitable on almost three days out of four. The tightest spreads lost money, and so did every version that simply followed recent trade prices. Adding two more seconds of delay made every configuration lose.

That cliff between one and three seconds bothered me. Real edges rarely appear and disappear that sharply.

## The two-second trap

The timestamps in Polymarket's API record when a trade settled on the Polygon blockchain. The trade itself was matched a little earlier, off-chain. If that gap is large, a quoter that "reacts after one second" in my simulation is actually reacting to Bitcoin moves that happened after the trade.

I checked this two ways. First, I shifted my fair value back and forth in time and looked at how well its changes explained changes in trade prices. The fit was best with a shift of three seconds into the past. That was either a real, slow market that a fast bot could exploit, or a clock problem in my data.

![Correlation between trade price changes and spot-model changes, by time shift](/images/polymarket-mm/timestamp-lag.png)

To find out which, I recorded one live market through Polymarket's websocket, which reports trades at the moment they are matched, and matched its 394 trades with the same trades in the API using their transaction hashes. The API was 2.2 seconds late on average. Add the half second it takes to align with one-second Binance candles, and the three-second shift is fully explained. The market reacts to Bitcoin in well under a second. My simulation had been quietly looking into the future.

With the delay corrected, the picture flips. Everything that made money did so only when it could see where Bitcoin went after the trade. At any realistic reaction time, every configuration lost. The best one, a wide quote with under a second of delay, still lost around $870 a day.

![Simulated quoter P&L per day against real quote latency](/images/polymarket-mm/latency.png)

## What it means

I set out to tune a quoter for these markets, and the backtest pointed somewhere else entirely. The spread is wide and it does get captured, but by whoever is fastest. A bot that needs even a second to react gets filled precisely when it would rather not be. The rebates keep the professional makers slightly ahead, and the takers, paying close to two percent in fees on average, fund all of it.

The lesson I will keep is about the timestamps. A delay of two seconds produced a strategy that won three days out of four, and confirming it took one live market and about ten minutes. I now check the clock before I trust any result that depends on speed.

## How I did it

Trades and outcomes come from Polymarket's public Data and Gamma APIs, and Bitcoin prices from Binance at one-second resolution. Down-token trades are converted into the equivalent trade on the Up token, so each market has a single price. The fair value treats Bitcoin as a random walk, uses the previous half hour of volatility, and applies the last-minute rule described above. Confidence intervals come from resampling whole markets, since trades within one market share the same outcome; the makers' result at resolution is not distinguishable from zero without rebates. The simulation works at one-second resolution, assumes my quote is first in line at its price, and approximates rebates as a fifth of the fee each trade generated. It covers a single month, because the API only keeps about that much history. The code and the data are on GitHub.
